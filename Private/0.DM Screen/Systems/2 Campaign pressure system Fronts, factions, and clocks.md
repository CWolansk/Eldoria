# 

A **front** is a threat that advances if the PCs ignore it. This comes from Dungeon World. A front has dangers, **grim portents**, and an **impending doom**. Grim portents are the visible steps showing what happens if a danger goes unchecked; the impending doom is the terrible result if the threat fully succeeds.

Think of fronts as your campaign’s “background simulation.”

## Front template

For each major threat, write:

**Name:** The Ashen Choir  
**Type:** Cult, empire, dragon, crime syndicate, plague, rival adventuring party  
**Goal:** Open the sealed angel-tomb  
**Impulse:** To purify the world through holy fire  
**Resources:** Fanatics, relics, spies in the church  
**Grim portents:**

1. A village shrine begins bleeding ash.
2. The bishop disappears.
3. The Choir seizes the cathedral.
4. The angel-tomb opens.

**Impending doom:** A burning seraph is released and declares judgment on the region.  
**How PCs notice:** Rumors, refugees, dreams, bounty notices, faction messengers.

You can run an entire campaign with three active fronts. After each session, ask:

> Which front advanced? Which front was delayed? Which front changed because of the PCs?

This gives your world motion without forcing a plot.

For more granular tracking, use **progress clocks** from Blades in the Dark. A clock is a circle divided into segments used to track progress against an obstacle or the approach of trouble. Blades suggests 4 segments for complex obstacles, 6 for complicated ones, and 8 for daunting ones.

Example campaign clocks:

**Cult opens the tomb:** 0/8  
**Duke’s suspicion of the party:** 0/6  
**Goblin tribe prepares raid:** 0/4  
**The party gains the queen’s trust:** 0/6

Clocks are excellent because they stop you from thinking in binary pass/fail terms.
